Faucet Repair in Austin, TX

Faucet repairs for drips, leaks, loose handles, and low water flow.

Call (512) 592-6789 Ask a question

People call us when the kitchen faucet will not stop dripping, the bathroom handle turns without doing much, or water is coming from around the base instead of the spout. They may say the faucet is “acting up,” “making a squeal,” or “barely putting out water.” Those everyday descriptions can point to a worn cartridge, damaged washer, loose handle connection, blocked aerator, failing supply line, or valve problem beneath the sink.

We check the fixture itself as well as the water connections that feed it. A drip from the spout is different from water collecting around the escutcheon plate, and a weak stream at one sink is different from low pressure throughout the house. The faucet make, handle style, room, and location of the shutoff valves help us narrow down the fault before a part is removed.

A minor drip may be able to wait briefly when the faucet shuts off and the cabinet below stays dry, but an active leak under the sink should not wait. Water from a supply connection, shutoff valve, or faucet body can soak cabinet panels, damage flooring, and reach the wall behind the fixture.

Dripping faucet and squealing handle

A faucet that drips from the spout after the handle is off often has a worn cartridge, stem seal, washer, or valve seat. The drip may be a slow drop every few seconds or a thin stream that continues after the handle is turned down. A single-handle kitchen faucet and a two-handle bathroom fixture use different internal parts, even when the symptom sounds the same.

A squealing, grinding, or stiff handle can come from mineral buildup around the cartridge, a dry stem, or a damaged handle adapter. If the handle spins freely without changing the water, the connection between the handle and stem may be stripped. If it is hard to move only when water is running, the cartridge or internal seals may be binding under water pressure.

Other calls involve water spraying sideways from the spout, a stream that has become weak, or water appearing around the faucet base whenever the fixture is used. A clogged aerator can restrict the stream at the tip, while a leaking base can indicate worn seals or a failed hose connection inside the faucet body.

These symptoms can look alike from the counter, but they are not one repair with one predictable scope. We inspect the faucet, hardware, and connections to separate a simple aerator or handle issue from a cartridge, valve, hose, or fixture problem that requires different parts and labor.

Fixture inspection

At the visit, we start with the faucet handle, spout, base, sprayer if fitted, and the cabinet below the sink. We run hot and cold water separately, look for drips at the spout and water at the mounting plate, and check whether the handle has normal travel. We also inspect the supply tubes, compression nuts, and angle stops under the fixture for moisture, corrosion, or movement.

We confirm whether the shutoff valves close fully before opening the faucet. Household water pressure is commonly kept in a range around 40 to 80 psi; pressure that is too high can shorten the life of cartridges, supply lines, and seals. A basic operational check and visible leak inspection may take 15 to 30 minutes, while disassembling a corroded faucet or tracing a leak inside a pull-down hose takes longer.

When the correct cartridge, washer, O-ring, aerator, or handle hardware is available and the shutoff works, we can often complete that repair during the same visit. We reassemble the faucet, run both temperature sides, check the base and cabinet connections, and confirm that the spout shuts off properly. A specialty cartridge, discontinued trim piece, damaged faucet body, or seized shutoff valve may require a return visit after the needed part or additional valve work is arranged.

Cartridge age and shutoff valves

The age and design of the faucet matter because cartridges, stems, seals, and handle adapters are not interchangeable across makes and models. A common cartridge in a current fixture is usually more straightforward to identify than an older faucet with discontinued internal parts. Mineral deposits can also make a cartridge difficult to remove without damaging the valve body or trim.

Access below the sink changes the scope of the work. A clear cabinet with visible supply lines and working angle stops allows us to isolate the faucet directly. A packed vanity, tight pedestal sink, corroded compression nut, or shutoff valve that will not close can add work before the faucet can be safely opened.

The condition of the shutoff valve is especially important on a faucet repair. If the valve is seized, leaking at the stem, or fails to stop water, the faucet cannot simply be taken apart as planned. We may need to address the valve or arrange for a replacement before completing the internal faucet repair.

Parts availability also affects the job. Some faucets have a model-specific cartridge, side spray hose, pull-down hose, or mounting component that must be matched to the manufacturer and ordered. A fixture with an accessible standard part generally involves less uncertainty than a faucet with no visible model marking or a part that is no longer readily available.

Model number and fixture room

Before calling, check which room contains the faucet and whether it is the kitchen sink, bathroom sink, bar sink, laundry sink, tub, or shower fixture. Let us know whether the problem is a drip from the spout, water under the cabinet, a loose handle, a weak stream, or a leak around the base, and whether water is running now.

If you can find a make or model number, keep it handy. The brand name may be printed on the faucet body, handle cap, escutcheon plate, spray head, or an identification tag on the supply hose beneath the sink. A photo of the whole fixture and the cabinet connections can also help identify the cartridge or hose style.

Tell us roughly when the fixture was installed, whether either shutoff valve works, and whether the cabinet has clear access around the supply lines.
