Leak Repair in Austin, TX

We trace and repair active leaks in pipes, fixtures, and concealed supply lines.

Call (512) 592-6789 Ask a question

A drip beneath a kitchen sink can leave a cabinet floor swollen within a week, while a slow leak behind a toilet may soften drywall or stain the ceiling below over a month. We often find that the visible water is not the full path of the leak: moisture can run along a supply line, framing member, or pipe sleeve before it reaches the spot where it finally appears.

A small stain that stays dry between uses may point to a drain connection, a shower valve, or a pipe that only leaks when pressure changes. A pipe leak that begins as a faint hiss or a damp patch can become a steady loss of water when a fitting loosens further, corrosion opens a pinhole, or a worn washer gives out.

We check the fixture, pipe, valve, and nearby surfaces to locate the source rather than simply drying the water. An active leak should not wait because water continues moving into the cabinet, wall cavity, flooring, or ceiling until the failed connection or damaged pipe is repaired.

Dripping pipe and wet cabinet

Customers commonly call us about a dripping pipe under the sink, a wet cabinet floor, or water collecting around the base of a toilet. A visible drip at a shutoff valve may occur only after the handle has been moved, while a supply hose can seep continuously at its threaded connection.

A brown ceiling spot, bubbling paint, or a damp area beneath an upstairs bathroom can mean water is escaping from a drain, tub overflow, shower valve, or pipe joint above the ceiling. We also hear reports of a hissing sound inside a wall, a water meter that moves when every fixture is off, or a musty smell near a vanity or laundry box.

Water around a faucet base can come from a loose mounting seal, a damaged supply connection, or a crack in the fixture body. Water near a water line in a garage, crawlspace, or exterior wall can come from pipe corrosion, a failed coupling, or condensation that needs to be separated from a true leak.

These symptoms can look alike from the room side, but the repair can involve very different parts, access points, and labor. We inspect the actual pipe, fitting, valve, or fixture so the work is based on the source of the water rather than the stain it left behind.

Supply-line callout and pressure test

We start by tracing the water from the visible drip or stain back to the fixture connection, shutoff valve, drain joint, or supply pipe. We inspect cabinet bases, wall penetrations, access panels, pipe insulation, and the area below the suspected leak. An initial inspection commonly takes about 20 to 40 minutes when the pipework and fixture connections are accessible.

We may use the water meter, a pressure gauge, moisture readings, and controlled fixture testing to narrow down the source. Residential water pressure is commonly kept in the range of about 40 to 80 psi, and unusually high pressure can put added stress on supply hoses, valve seals, and appliance connections. We also check whether a leak occurs only when a faucet runs, a toilet refills, a shower valve is open, or the main water line remains pressurized.

For an exposed connection, we can often replace a supply hose, compression fitting, washer, valve stem, or short damaged section of pipe during the same visit when the needed material is available. A pipe behind tile, a finished wall, a slab, or a cabinet back may require an access opening, drying work, or a return visit after a specific fitting or replacement valve is obtained. If a repair affects concealed piping or requires building work, we explain the access and any required sign-off before that work begins.

Shutoff valves and wall access

The age and condition of the failed part matter because an older angle stop, galvanized pipe, corroded threaded fitting, or brittle plastic connection may not separate cleanly. A leak at one visible nut can reveal corrosion on the valve body or pipe stub behind it, which changes the scope from a small connection repair to replacement of the affected section.

Access also moves the work. A supply hose in an open vanity is different from a leaking elbow behind a tiled shower wall, beneath hardwood flooring, or above a finished ceiling. Existing access panels, crawlspace clearance, cabinet openings, and the route of the pipe all affect how the leak can be reached and repaired.

A seized shutoff valve can prevent isolation at the fixture, and a valve that will not close may need replacement before the leaking connection can be worked on. Specialty cartridges, discontinued fixture parts, matching trim, and unusual pipe materials may need to be ordered. Where a concealed pipe repair involves code-required approval or sign-off, that requirement also affects the work planned around the repair.

Fixture model and access panel

Before calling, note which room and fixture is involved, such as the upstairs hall bathroom toilet, the kitchen sink, or the laundry supply box. If the leak is at a faucet, shower valve, or toilet, look for the make and model number on the fixture body, tank label, trim plate, or installation paperwork. Tell us whether water is dripping now, only appears when the fixture runs, or has been shut off at a nearby valve. If there is an access panel, cabinet opening, crawlspace entry, or ceiling stain below the leak, let us know where it is and whether it can be reached.
