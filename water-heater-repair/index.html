Water Heater Repair in Austin, TX

No hot water, tank leaks, burner faults, and element repairs across Austin.

Call (512) 592-6789 Ask a question

Two different failures often arrive under the same water heater repair label. From the hallway, a dry utility closet and a shower that turns cold point toward a heating or control fault; water at the drain pan, closet threshold, or garage floor points toward a leak at the tank, valve, pipe, or connection. We handle both conditions throughout Austin, Rollingwood, West Lake Hills, and Lost Creek.

A tank can also make a short burst of hot water before going cold, while a tankless unit may stop heating and show an error code. Those symptoms can come from a worn heating element, thermostat, burner component, sediment buildup, failed sensor, or a water supply issue, so we inspect the heater itself instead of treating every cold shower as the same repair.

A dry heater that has simply stopped producing hot water can usually wait for a planned diagnostic visit. An active tank leak, water discharging from a relief valve, burning odor, or gas odor should not wait, because water can damage flooring and framing while a fuel or electrical fault can create a more serious hazard around the heater.

No hot water or rumbling

“No hot water” is the most common description we hear, but it can mean the water is fully cold, only lukewarm, or hot for a few minutes before the temperature drops. An electric tank may have one failed element while the other still works, and a gas unit may have a burner or ignition problem that prevents the tank from reheating.

Customers also call about a water heater that is leaking, a puddle under the tank, or water coming from the pipe at the top of the unit. A loose connection, aging valve, temperature-and-pressure relief valve, corroded nipple, or a tank seam can all put water in the same pan or on the same floor.

Popping, rumbling, or banging from the tank often points to sediment moving at the bottom of the heater. A pilot that will not stay lit, a burner that clicks without lighting, rusty hot water, or an error message on a tankless display each sends us toward a different set of parts and tests.

These reports can sound alike from the kitchen or hallway, but the repair can involve very different components and different amounts of labor. Looking at the tank, burner, wiring, valves, and water connections is what separates a small replaceable part from a larger heater problem.

Tank and burner inspection

At the callout, we start with the tank, drain pan, supply pipes, shutoff valve, and visible connections. We check for active moisture, corrosion, staining, and relief-valve discharge, then verify the hot-water temperature at a nearby faucet. A straightforward diagnosis commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes, though a concealed leak, intermittent tankless error, or difficult attic installation can take longer to trace.

We measure the incoming water pressure rather than assuming the plumbing is within the usual residential range of 40 to 80 psi. On a tank heater, we check the thermostat settings, electrical connections, heating elements, burner assembly, ignition parts, venting, and the condition of the anode system where access allows. The temperature-and-pressure relief valve uses a 3/4-inch discharge line, and its routing and termination matter when we assess whether the valve or the installation needs correction.

When the fault is a serviceable thermostat, element, igniter, flame sensor, thermocouple, valve, or connection and the needed part is available, the repair is often completed during the same visit. A leaking tank, failed tankless heat exchanger, seized shutoff, ordered part, replacement unit, or work requiring permit review and inspection is normally arranged as follow-up work after we identify the correct scope.

Tank age, valve, and access

The repair charge depends heavily on the condition and age of the tank, the part that has failed, and how easily we can reach the heater. A newer electric tank with an accessible element panel is a different job from an older tank with corrosion around the nipples, a leaking seam, or a buried drain connection.

Access changes the work when the heater sits in a tight closet, attic platform, garage alcove, or pan with limited room around the pipes. A shutoff valve that closes normally lets us isolate the unit cleanly; a seized valve, brittle supply line, or corroded fitting can add related plumbing work before the heater can be repaired safely.

Special-order control boards, burner assemblies, tankless sensors, and model-specific parts can also change the scope. If the installation needs a replacement unit, vent changes, electrical changes, or municipal sign-off, we explain those items separately from a straightforward component repair.

Model plate and tank access

Before calling, locate the model and serial number on the manufacturer plate, usually printed on the side of a tank jacket or on the side or underside of a tankless unit.

Let us know whether the heater is gas, electric, or tankless, approximately how old it is, and which room, closet, garage, or attic holds the unit.

Mention whether water is actively running into the pan or onto the floor, whether the burner or display has an error message, and whether the hot water is cold at every fixture or only at one shower.

A photo of the model plate, pipes above the tank, and the access path can help us identify likely parts before the visit.
