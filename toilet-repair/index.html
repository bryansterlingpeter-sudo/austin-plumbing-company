Toilet Repair in Austin, TX

Toilet repair for running tanks, weak flushes, leaks, and loose fixtures in Austin.

Call (512) 592-6789 Ask a question

A plunger and a few firm strokes are a sensible first response when a toilet backs up or gives a weak flush. A plunger can clear a paper blockage in the trapway, and checking that the tank handle still moves is worthwhile before calling. The limit comes when the bowl fills but will not empty, the tank keeps refilling after a flush, or the water level rises with every attempt.

Inside the tank, a flapper, fill valve, chain, overflow tube, or flush handle can stop working without much warning. At the base of the bowl, a failed wax ring, loose closet bolts, or a damaged flange can leave water on the floor even when the tank parts appear normal. We repair toilet faults in Austin, Rollingwood, West Lake Hills, and Lost Creek by checking the fixture from the supply valve to the drain connection.

A toilet repair should not be left for long when water is running, pooling at the bowl, or threatening to overflow. A continuously filling tank can waste water, while a leak below the toilet can soak flooring and reach the subfloor beneath the bathroom.

Toilet keeps running, won’t flush

We regularly hear that the toilet keeps running after the handle is released, that the bowl fills too high before draining, or that the handle goes down with no flush at all. A weak flush can leave paper in the bowl because the tank is not releasing enough water, while a toilet that refills every few minutes may have water slipping past the flapper or running into the overflow tube.

Water around the toilet base, a sewer smell near the bowl, or a fixture that rocks when someone sits down are different symptoms again. A loose bowl can point to closet bolts or a flange issue, and an odor may come from a compromised seal rather than the tank. These problems can look similar from the bathroom floor, but the failed part, the labor involved, and the repair cost can be quite different once we inspect the toilet.

Fixture inspection

During a fixture inspection, we remove the tank lid, operate the flush handle, and watch the fill valve, flapper, chain, and overflow tube through a full flush cycle. We check whether the water level reaches the marked line in the tank, test the shutoff valve, inspect the supply line, and look around the bowl base for movement or moisture. An initial check commonly takes 15 to 30 minutes, depending on whether the fault is visible in the tank or requires pulling the toilet from the flange.

We can often replace common tank parts such as a flapper, fill valve, handle, chain, or supply line during the visit when the correct part is available. If the bowl needs to be lifted, we inspect the wax ring, closet bolts, and flange before resetting the toilet. A cracked bowl, damaged flange, unusual flush valve, or model-specific tank component may need an ordered part or a separate return visit, and we explain what the inspection shows before that work is arranged.

Shutoff valves and tank parts

The age and type of the tank parts affect the repair because a standard flapper or fill valve is handled differently from a model-specific flush assembly. Older hardware can be brittle, corroded, or no longer made in the same form, which can change whether a direct replacement is practical. A toilet with a concealed mounting system or an uncommon tank lid may also require a particular component rather than a universal part.

Access around the bowl matters when we need to reach closet bolts, the supply valve, or the flange beneath the toilet. A shutoff valve that has seized can add work because we may need to isolate water before replacing a supply connection or tank component. If a damaged drain connection, altered piping, or building requirement needs sign-off, that also affects the scope beyond a straightforward tank repair.

Tank model and bathroom access

When you call, we ask for the toilet’s bathroom location and whether it is the only fixture affected. We can use a photo of a model number printed inside the tank, beneath the tank lid, or on the porcelain to identify the right flush or fill components. We also ask whether water is running into the bowl now, whether the base is wet, and whether the handle still moves. Let us know if the toilet sits in a tight powder room, behind a cabinet, or on an upper floor with limited access.
